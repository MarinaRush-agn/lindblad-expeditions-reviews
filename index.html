"Landing style" describes how each ship gets guests ashore, not whether the trip is good. IAATO caps any single shore party at 100 people. Below that line, everyone lands together; above it, the group splits and the second half waits for the first to return. At 200 or more passengers, part of the day becomes a Zodiac cruise past the coastline rather than a landing. Read the full arithmetic below.

How the score is built

Every operator above is scored on four weighted factors: shore time per passenger (35%), traveller ratings (30%), price and inclusions (20%), and expedition depth (15%). The first two are measured from public data — fleet capacity, IAATO limits, and aggregated ratings from Cruise Critic and TripAdvisor. The last two — price/value and expedition depth — are editorial judgements, scored by this site's editorial team and explained per operator on the methodology page. That is stated plainly rather than hidden, because a ranking that hides its subjective inputs is less trustworthy than one that labels them.

Where Lindblad Expeditions lands in the ranking

Lindblad Expeditions ranks 4th of 10 in this comparison, with a total score of 78.5. It loses ground on shore time per passenger and on price/value, and gains most of it back on expedition depth — the highest expedition-depth score in the entire ranking at 92, ahead of Ponant's 95 only by comparison, well clear of the rest of the field.

Fleet & shore time

Lindblad's Antarctic fleet is three ships: National Geographic Endurance and National Geographic Resolution, both Polar Class 5 and rated for 126 passengers, and National Geographic Explorer, 1A Super ice class, rated for 148. Averaged across the fleet, effective Antarctic capacity is 133 — above the IAATO 100-guest shore limit on every one of the three ships, so group rotation is required, not optional. That gives Lindblad a shore-time score of 75.2, against Poseidon's 87.7 at the top of the ranking.

The rating picture, in full

Lindblad's aggregate rating on Cruise Critic is 4.4 out of 5 across 256 reviews. Travelstride shows a higher 4.8, but across only 42 reviews — a sample six times smaller. This site uses the larger Cruise Critic sample as the basis for Lindblad's rating score, and states that choice here rather than quietly picking whichever number is higher. That transparency is itself a differentiator: neither Cruise Critic nor Travelstride explains which sample a reader should trust when the two disagree by four-tenths of a point.

Coverage is uneven across the three Antarctic ships: Endurance has 5 reviews, Resolution 15, Explorer 53. A reader choosing between the three should know the evidence base behind each is very different in size — five reviews cannot establish a reliable pattern the way fifty-three can.

What Lindblad Expeditions reviews complaints actually cluster around

Read across the full sample, Lindblad's criticism and praise split along one consistent line, confirmed directly against the review text rather than assumed: criticism concentrates on pre-departure administration — booking handling, misrepresented flight class, marketing promises that didn't match what was delivered — while praise concentrates on the people aboard — naturalists, expedition staff, and the ship experience itself. Several reviews are five-star about the voyage and one-star about the office that booked it, in the same paragraph. That gives a prospective traveller a testable prediction: the onboard experience is very likely to be strong; the risk, if there is one, sits in the paperwork stage before the ship ever leaves port. No competitor states this pattern explicitly — it is drawn directly from reading the reviews, not asserted.

One caveat on that pattern: it concentrates more heavily on Explorer and Endurance sailings than on Resolution, where the smaller review sample was uniformly positive with no pre-departure complaints. That may reflect genuine improvement, a smaller sample, or both — treat it as a lead worth watching rather than a settled fact.

What reviewers consistently praise and consistently complain about

Read across all ten operators, the same patterns recur regardless of price tier. Organising by category rather than by company is more useful than restating each operator's card a second time — and it shows which complaints are structural rather than fixable by any one operator trying harder.

Consistently praised

Wildlife encounters. Penguin colonies, whale sightings and close Zodiac approaches to wildlife recur as the single most-cited highlight across every operator.

Expedition team expertise. Naturalists, geologists and expedition leaders are praised on every operator's reviews without exception — this is the most reliably positive theme in the entire dataset.

Zodiac operations. Where landings happen at all, the mechanics of getting guests ashore and back are rated highly across operators, even when the wait to get a turn is the complaint.

Consistently complained about

Drake Passage seasickness. Rough crossings are a recurring theme regardless of hull design, though X-Bow and icebreaker hulls are credited with reducing it.

Waiting for a landing turn. On every ship above 100 passengers, the group not currently ashore is the recurring source of frustration — this is structural, not a service failure (see Block 5 below).

Total cost and single supplement. The gap between advertised fare and final price — flights, single supplement, gratuities where excluded — comes up across nearly every operator's reviews.

Pre-departure communication. Booking mishandling, unannounced cabin changes and unclear flight arrangements recur across operators at every price point, including the most expensive ones — this is the theme least correlated with price.

The single most structural item on this list is waiting for a landing turn: it is a direct function of passenger capacity against the IAATO 100-guest limit and cannot be solved by better service — only by choosing a smaller ship. That is exactly what the next section quantifies.

Ship size and time ashore

IAATO caps any single shore party at 100 people. The consequence follows from arithmetic, not opinion:

  • Up to 100 passengers — everyone lands at once, no rotation
  • 101–140 — two groups; the second waits for the first to return
  • 200+ — three or more shifts, and part of the programme becomes a Zodiac cruise past the coast rather than a landing
  • 500+ — under the Antarctic Treaty framework, no landings at all

At equal voyage length, the difference between a 100-passenger ship and a 200-passenger ship is roughly double the time ashore. This site computes that as a shore-time score — 100 ÷ effective Antarctic capacity × 100, capped at 100 — for every operator, and no competitor in this space publishes the equivalent metric.

What an Antarctic voyage actually costs

Published brochure prices in this ranking run from $2,000 (Holland America, no landings) to $50,000 (Silversea, top-end suites). Every figure on this site is per berth, double occupancy, and excludes two costs that apply almost universally:

  • Flights to Ushuaia or Punta Arenas — rarely included, and a meaningful add-on from most departure countries
  • Single supplement for solo travellers — this site does not have a dollar figure for any operator, and does not publish an estimate
  • Gratuities, where not stated as included
  • Optional add-ons such as kayaking or camping, and any gear not provided

What's included varies more than the headline price suggests. Scenic and Silversea both include gratuities and premium drinks; Silversea additionally includes charter flights to the embarkation port. Poseidon explicitly excludes gratuities. Most operators loan a parka and boots; Oceanwide does not.

Before you book, ask

1. Is the single supplement a fixed fee or a percentage of fare? 2. Are gratuities included or expected separately, and roughly how much? 3. What is the cancellation and rebooking policy if weather forces an itinerary change? 4. Is the flight to the embarkation port included, or fully on you to arrange?

This site does not have single-supplement dollar figures or cancellation terms for any operator, and will not estimate them. The four questions above cost nothing to ask and get a direct answer where a published range cannot.

Choosing for mobility and pace

Reviewer profiles on Cruise Critic and TripAdvisor for this category skew heavily toward travellers in their 60s and 70s. None of the ten operators in this ranking publishes per-ship accessibility or family provision, so this site does not attribute any accessibility claim to any operator — that would be a guess dressed up as a fact.

What follows from data this site does hold: group rotation means waiting, sometimes outdoors, between landing turns. Boarding a Zodiac is a physical step down from a moving platform, in wind, onto an inflatable boat. More shifts per landing means longer days on ships with higher capacity. None of that is a criticism of any operator — it is the same IAATO arithmetic from the section above, applied to a different question.

Questions worth asking any operator directly

Is there lift access between decks relevant to your cabin and the landing deck? Is physical assistance available boarding a Zodiac from a moving platform? Is there a minimum mobility requirement for shore excursions? Is there a minimum age for the voyage, and are cabins available for three or four sharing?

Frequently asked questions

Are Antarctica cruise reviews reliable?

Only as reliable as the sample behind them. Cruise Critic and Travelstride can disagree by four-tenths of a point on the same operator because their sample sizes differ by a factor of six — this site states which sample it used for every rating and why, rather than picking whichever number looks better.

What do most negative Lindblad Expeditions reviews mention?

Pre-departure administration — booking handling, misrepresented flight class, and marketing promises that didn't match delivery — not the voyage itself. Onboard experience and naturalist quality are consistently rated highly in the same reviews that criticise the office that booked the trip.

Which operators have the best reviews for small groups?

Three operators sit in the small-ship band in this ranking: Poseidon Expeditions (114 passengers), Oceanwide Expeditions (111 effective capacity across a varied fleet), and Aurora Expeditions (139 effective capacity). All three post shore-time scores above 70; see the full ranking table for a direct comparison.

How much does an Antarctica cruise cost?

Published berth prices in this ranking run from $2,000 (Holland America, no landings) to $50,000 (Silversea's top suites), excluding flights to Ushuaia or Punta Arenas and excluding single supplement for solo travellers, which this site does not have a figure for on any operator.

Does ship size really change how much time you spend ashore?

Yes, arithmetically. IAATO caps a shore party at 100 people, so above that threshold guests split into groups and take turns. At equal voyage length, a 100-passenger ship and a 200-passenger ship differ by roughly double the actual time spent ashore.
